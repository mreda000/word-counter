Use Word Counts to Plan an Essay or Brief

Published by ChickenTask · Updated November 21, 2026

A word limit is a space budget. It can help you decide how much room to give an explanation, example, or recommendation. It does not tell you whether your argument is accurate or useful. Start with what your reader needs, then use the count to fit that purpose into the available space.

1. Establish what the limit includes

Before drafting an assignment, application, or brief, check its instructions. A title, heading, quotation, reference list, or footnote may be included or excluded. Paste only the material that the instructions count into the Word Counter & Text Analyzer. Keep a complete copy of your document elsewhere.

ChickenTask counts groups separated by whitespace. A number such as 2026 counts as one word; well-planned also counts as one. Your submission platform may use different rules, so check the final document there as well. See the counting rules and limitations.

2. Allocate space to the decision

Suppose you need a 600-word brief comparing two ways to organize a class project. Your reader needs enough background to understand the choice, evidence about both options, and a clear recommendation. The following is an example planning budget, not a rule for every assignment.

The second plan makes room for the answer the reader came for. To check a section, copy it into the counter on its own. The tool counts the whole editor; it does not automatically identify your headings or allocate a budget to each section.

3. Revise for coverage before cutting sentences

  1. List the required points. Mark where each one is answered in the draft. A short piece that omits the main question still needs work.
  2. Remove repeated explanations. If the same background appears in the introduction and conclusion, decide where it helps most.
  3. Protect evidence. Keep the dates, numbers, conditions, and qualifications that make the recommendation reliable.
  4. Measure again. Use Copy results to record the revised count, then read the complete document in its original layout.

If you are well below the limit, look for an unanswered question or a missing example. Add useful detail where it is needed; reaching the maximum is not a goal by itself.

Choose a useful length for online writing

There is no universal 300–600-word length that makes a page rank better. Google's guidance on helpful content explicitly says it has no preferred word count. A short answer may fully solve one task; a complicated comparison may need considerably more space. Use the length that lets you deliver the promised answer clearly.

Put the plan into practice

Open the word counter, measure your complete draft, and then measure its largest section. Ask whether that section deserves that much of the reader's attention. For sentence-level changes, continue with five ways to edit without losing meaning. If you are preparing a talk, use the separate speech timing worksheet.
