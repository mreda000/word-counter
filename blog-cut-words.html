5 Ways to Cut Words Without Losing Meaning

Published by ChickenTask · Updated November 21, 2026

A useful edit preserves what the reader needs to know. Word count can show the size of a change, but a shorter sentence is not automatically clearer. Keep a copy of the original, make one change at a time, and check that the reader can still identify the action, the people involved, and any conditions.

A complete example: 28 words to 18

Before — 28 words:

I am writing to let you know that the team will hold a meeting on Friday at 10 a.m. in Room 4 to discuss the updated project schedule.

After — 18 words:

The team will meet on Friday at 10 a.m. in Room 4 to discuss the updated project schedule.

The revised notice removes an eight-word introduction and changes “hold a meeting” to “meet.” It still tells the reader who, when, where, and why. Paste each version separately into the word counter to check the totals. The abbreviation a.m. affects the simple sentence counter, so use the word result for this comparison.

1. Replace long phrases when the meaning matches

Before: “Due to the fact that the meeting starts at noon, please arrive by 11:50.” After: “Because the meeting starts at noon, please arrive by 11:50.” This changes 14 words to 10 while preserving the reason and arrival time.

Read the full sentence after a substitution. “Because,” “although,” and “after” express different relationships; a shorter connector is useful only when it conveys the intended one.

2. Let a verb carry the action

“We made a decision to postpone the launch” becomes “We decided to postpone the launch”: eight words become six. Similar edits can remove unnecessary combinations such as “conduct a review of” when “review” says what you mean.

Do not remove a person or organization that the reader needs to identify. “The launch was postponed” may be shorter, but it no longer says who made the decision.

3. Remove repeated meanings carefully

“Please reply back with your final decision” can become “Please reply with your decision,” reducing seven words to five. Here, “back” adds little. Keep “final” if you need to distinguish a confirmed decision from an earlier provisional answer.

This check applies to whole paragraphs too. If the introduction already gives the deadline, a later reminder should add something useful, such as the submission link or what to do if the deadline cannot be met.

4. Put the request first

“I am writing to ask whether you can send the report today” becomes “Can you send the report today?” That changes 12 words to six. A direct request works well when the context is already clear.

Keep context when it helps the recipient act. If they manage several reports, name the report. If “today” would be ambiguous across time zones, give the date and time. Politeness and precision can justify extra words.

5. Replace vague emphasis with useful information

Compare “The update is really very important for all users” with “All users need the update to keep file exports working.” The second version is actually one word longer: ten instead of nine. It gives the reader a reason to act rather than relying on emphasis.

Only supply a reason you know is true. This example illustrates editing a fictional software notice; it is not a claim about a ChickenTask update.

Check what survived the edit

  • Are the dates, amounts, locations, and responsibilities still present?
  • Did words such as “may,” “only,” “before,” or “unless” carry a condition you accidentally removed?
  • Can a reader act on the revised version without asking for missing context?
  • Does the tone still fit the situation?

Measure the revision with the word counter, then read both versions side by side. For an entire essay or brief, use the section planning example before trimming individual sentences.
