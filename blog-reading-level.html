How to Interpret a Reading Level Estimate

Published by ChickenTask · Updated November 21, 2026

ChickenTask's estimated English reading grade is a rough editing signal. It is calculated from sentence length and estimated syllables. It cannot tell whether a reader knows your subject, whether an instruction is complete, or whether the information is correct. Use it to find a passage worth inspecting, then judge the actual wording.

What goes into the estimate?

The tool uses a Flesch–Kincaid-style calculation:

0.39 × (English words ÷ sentence segments)
+ 11.8 × (estimated syllables ÷ English words)
− 15.59

For this calculation, ChickenTask strips each whitespace-separated token down to letters A–Z and discards tokens with no such letters. It estimates syllables by counting groups of English vowel letters after removing a final “e,” with a minimum of one for a nonempty word. The result is rounded to a whole number and cannot fall below zero. When there are no usable English words or sentence segments, it shows N/A.

This is a simplified implementation. English pronunciation is irregular, and punctuation-based sentence splitting can mishandle abbreviations or decimals. The score is especially unsuitable for short fragments, lists, code, non-English writing, and specialized terms that the reader already knows.

Try a worked comparison

Original — 11 words:

Before the implementation of this procedure, please provide the additional documentation.

Revision — 9 words:

Before using this process, please send the extra documents.

With the current word counter, these examples produce approximate grades 17 and 8. The change reflects shorter, more familiar wording in this particular calculation. It does not prove that one passage requires a specific educational qualification or that the revision is understandable to every reader.

Both sentences still leave a practical question unanswered: which documents? A complete instruction might need a list or a link. Adding that detail could increase the word count while making the instruction more useful.

Make an editing decision, not a score decision

  1. Identify the reader's task. Are they choosing an option, following a process, or learning a concept?
  2. Locate the difficult passage. Look for a long chain of conditions, an undefined term, or a sentence carrying several separate actions.
  3. Rewrite one part. Put the main action first, explain a necessary term, or split steps into a list.
  4. Check accuracy. Keep qualifications and essential technical vocabulary. Define a term if substituting a simpler word would change its meaning.
  5. Try the instruction with a reader. Ask what they would do next and where they need more information. Their answer is more useful than chasing a particular grade number.

Why a lower number can be misleading

“Send it there” is short and uses simple words, but it is unhelpful when the reader does not know what “it” and “there” refer to. A longer sentence that names the form, recipient, and deadline can be a better instruction. Likewise, a correct technical name may increase the estimate while giving an expert reader exactly the precision they need.

Do not use this estimate as an accessibility certification, a reading test, or a target that all pages must reach. Pair clear wording with useful headings, examples, and a logical order.
